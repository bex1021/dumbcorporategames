# Blocked — Story Bible

*The narrative through-line for the campaign. Everything here is derived from
the existing blueprints and shipped content, not invented alongside them —
sources are cited per section so this doc stays subordinate to them. If this
doc and a blueprint disagree, the blueprint wins and this doc is wrong.*

**Purpose:** one place that answers "what is this game about" for the website,
a store page, press, or anyone who asks. The blueprints describe *systems*.
This describes *the day*.

---

## Logline

An earnest project manager has one working day to keep a project nobody
understands from turning red — and discovers that every honest answer costs
more than the dishonest one.

## The one-sentence version, for people in a hurry

**Blocked** is one game about one day at one company, told in four genres,
because that is what the day actually feels like.

---

## Who Leonard is

**Leonard P.** Project manager at Alignly. Initials LP — they're on his Jira
tickets, his avatar, and eventually his fight card.

He is not a satirical idiot and he is not a hero. He is good at his job in the
only way the job permits: he is responsive, he is agreeable, he writes a clean
status update. He genuinely wants to help the five people who report status to
him, and at 9:00 AM he still believes that helping them and protecting the
project are the same activity.

They are not. The whole game is him finding that out.

The blueprint states his arc plainly, and the four stages are those four beats:

> **An earnest PM slowly becoming dead-inside.**
> 1. Starts the day genuinely trying to help.
> 2. Learns that every solution creates another meeting.
> 3. Begins using corporate language defensively.
> 4. By 4:30, can either survive the Executive Review or trigger total escalation.
>
> — `Blocked_Game_Blueprint.md`, "Player character"

What makes him worth playing is that he never becomes cynical *on purpose*.
Nobody corrupts him. He is simply presented, over and over, with a menu on
which the kind option is expensive and the corporate option is free, and a
clock that does not care which he picks.

## Who the Exec is

Nameless. Deliberately, for the entire campaign.

He is "Exec → You" in the Slack log. He is "THE EXEC," in quotation marks, on
the Phase 4 fight card — and the blueprint is explicit that *the quotation
marks are the joke*. He is not a villain. He never raises his voice. In the
finale, every one of his attacks is delivered in a calm, reasonable register.

He appears at 08:58, two minutes before the game starts, with the only two
messages that matter:

> **need CHP green by EOD. do whatever it takes.**
> **↑ this is between us**

That is the entire inciting incident. An impossible instruction and a request
for deniability, delivered in lowercase, before Leonard has taken his coat off.

## What's at stake

Not the project. Nobody in the fiction cares about the project, and the game's
final line says so.

What's at stake is **Leonard's standing** — which the finale eventually stops
being coy about and renders as a health bar named **CREDIBILITY**. Lose it and
the ending is a Performance Improvement Plan, delivered with a handshake and
the line *"We're really investing in your growth."* Win it and the Exec extends
the same hand and says *"Great sync. Ship it."*

The handshake animation is identical in both endings. That is on purpose.

And underneath that, the real stake: whether Leonard gets through the day
having done anything he'd want to describe to someone he likes.

## The project

**The Customer Happiness Portal Refresh.** Ticket prefix CHP.

Per the blueprint: *"No one fully understands it. Everyone agrees it is
important."* It never gets defined, in any stage, at any point. That is not a
gap in the writing — it is the subject of the writing.

## The company

**Alignly.** Their mission statement: *"Alignly helps teams align on
alignment."*

## The day

One continuous workday, 9:00 AM to 4:30 PM, played in four stages. The hours
between noon and 4:30 are deliberately unmodeled — the blueprint calls that
gap *"the implied corporate void."* Nothing happened. That is realistic.

| | Time | Where | What you do | Genre |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **I** | 9:00 – 10:15 AM | The bullpen | Walk to five people and answer them | Third-person walking sim |
| **II** | 10:45 – 11:00 AM | Leonard's monitor | Route four ticket updates | 8-bit endless runner |
| **III** | 11:00 AM – 12:00 PM | Across town, beige Camry | Get lunch, get the Exec's salmon bowl, get back | Driving game |
| **IV** | 4:30 PM | A conference room | Answer for all of it | One-on-one fighting game |

### The genre thesis — why four different games

This is the idea the whole thing rests on, and it's stated in the Phase 4
blueprint:

> The whole campaign has rendered office work as game genres — walking sim,
> endless runner, driving game. Phase 4 is the payoff of that pattern: the
> meeting every phase has dreaded, rendered as the genre of pure confrontation.
> **The joke completes itself: every corporate conversation was always a
> fighting game. Now the HUD admits it.**

The genre shifts are not variety for its own sake. Each one is an argument
about what that part of a workday actually is:

- Walking between desks to collect status **is** a walking sim. There is no
  more accurate genre for it.
- Clearing a ticket queue **is** an endless runner. It does not end. You do
  not win. You survive a stretch of it and then it is tomorrow.
- A lunch break with an errand attached **is** a driving game with a timer,
  and the errand is why it stops being a break.
- And the meeting where someone senior decides what you're worth **was
  always** a fighting game. The last stage doesn't transform the office. It
  just stops pretending.

**Critical tonal rule, locked in the blueprint: the office never transforms
and nobody acknowledges the genre.** Stage IV is a normal conference room at
4:30 PM. The Exec sits, then stands. The camera drops to a side-on fighting
framing, health bars slide in — and he calmly asks *"So. Where are we on the
portal refresh?"* while settling into a loose southpaw stance. No one comments.

## Storyboard — the four stages

### Stage I · Pre-Standup Alignment
**9:00 – 10:15 AM · the bullpen · walking sim**

Leonard arrives to a Slack channel where all five of his colleagues have
already posted "no blockers." Every one of them then describes, in the same
message, a blocker. *"no blockers (small ask coming separately)."* *"none here
— told the client thursday btw, ping me."*

Nobody is lying. This is just the format.

He has 75 minutes to walk the floor and talk to all five before standup. Each
conversation offers three or four responses and **all of them work**. Clarify
the requirement — it fixes the problem and costs 15 minutes he doesn't have.
File a ticket — converts despair into governance. Book a sync — which spawns a
sub-sync. Or say *"great, sounds like no blockers"* and keep the status green,
and two conversations later reality files a complaint.

The game shows the price of each option before he pays it. He is never
tricked. He just watches himself take the cheap one, because standup is in
forty minutes.

**Beat: he starts the day genuinely trying to help.**
**Ends on:** the dashboard is green. Nobody is okay. A meeting invite titled
"Pre-Pre-Standup-Retro Sync" appears on tomorrow's calendar. It is described
as *"informal."*

### Stage II · Jira Run
**10:45 – 11:00 AM · inside the monitor · 8-bit endless runner**

Standup is survived. Leonard sits down to log four updates and looks into the
backlog. The backlog looks back.

He falls into his own screen. What was a Kanban board becomes a side-scrolling
gauntlet — dependency walls that can't be jumped, gates that only open for the
right ticket, a sprint that keeps accelerating. The updates he's carrying are
the ones he invented an hour ago: **the Slack pings that chase him down the
track quote, word for word, what he told each person in Stage I.**

The day has started keeping receipts.

**Beat: he learns that every solution creates another meeting.**
**Ends on:** four updates deposited. The board is green. The work is not done;
the *reporting* is done, and the game is clear about the difference.

### Stage III · Lunch Dash
**11:00 AM – 12:00 PM · across town · driving game**

The first time all day he is alone. He walks out through the lobby, gets into
a beige Camry, and for about nine seconds it feels like escape.

Then the errand: his own lunch, and the Exec's salmon bowl from Corporate Slop
Bowlz, back at his desk by noon for the Architecture Sync. The radio plays ads
for companies that sound like his company. The bowl rides in the cupholder,
on camera, and it reacts to his driving — the blueprint's phrasing is *"the
salmon bowl is judging your driving."*

The fantasy is ten minutes of peace. The reality is that his one free hour has
an owner, and it isn't him.

How he arrives back is graded — **composed, functional, or disheveled** — and
that grade is not cosmetic. It is carried forward into the finale.

**Beat: he begins using corporate language defensively.**
**Ends on:** noon. He is back at his desk. He has eaten in the car. Nothing
was accomplished. Status: green.

### Stage IV · Performance Review
**4:30 PM · a conference room · fighting game**

The corporate void of the afternoon passes unrendered. Then it's 4:30, and the
Exec finally wants his update.

Three rounds, each themed as an agenda item. Leonard's health bar is
**CREDIBILITY**; the Exec's is **SKEPTICISM** — you are not hurting him, you
are wearing down his objections. *"Pushback:"* is a heavy punch. *"Let's take
this offline"* is a throw: Leonard hands over a calendar invite and the invite
explodes. Blocking is called **Active Listening**, and Leonard nods while he
does it.

Everything from the day is in the room. A composed lunch run unlocks a super
called **Proactive Framing**. A day spent making people angry changes how he
starts each round.

And below 25% credibility, one move becomes available, free, always, glowing:
**THROW UNDER THE BUS.** Leonard names a coworker. A bus passes outside. It
does enormous damage, it costs nothing, and it is permanently recorded —
capping the ending at a tarnished variant with an asterisk that is never
explained, and one line in the stat sheet: *"People sacrificed at the review:
1 (we have notes)."*

The horror is not that the move is punishing. It's that it's there, and free,
and available exactly when he's desperate enough to want it.

**Beat: he survives, or he doesn't.**
**Ends on:** a handshake. Either *"Great sync. Ship it."* or a PIP form and
*"We're really investing in your growth."* The animation is the same.

## The ending

Every ending in every stage, win or lose, closes on the same hardcoded line:

> ### Actual Business Value Generated: **$0.00**

It is never explained, never referenced by any character, and never softened.
It is the only moment in the game that speaks in the player's voice instead of
the company's.

## Themes, stated plainly

**The work was never the win condition.** Leonard is scored all day on
alignment, status, and sentiment. He is scored at no point on whether the
Customer Happiness Portal Refresh got better. It does not get better. Nobody
notices.

**Every option is technically correct.** The game has no trap answers and no
gotchas. Each choice genuinely helps the project on paper. The costs are all
somewhere else — on the humans, on the calendar, on next week. The player is
never fooled; they're just on a clock.

**Consequences arrive late, in someone else's voice.** What Leonard says at
9:15 comes back as a Slack ping at 10:50 and as a stance in a fight at 4:30.
Nothing is forgotten and nothing is confronted directly.

**Nobody is a villain.** Not the Exec, not the coworkers, not HR. Everyone is
professional, sincere, and slightly trapped. The blueprint's tone rule: *the
more absurd things get, the more corporate the language becomes.*

## Voice — how to write about this game

- Deadpan. Never wink. The moment the game acknowledges it's being funny, it
  stops being funny.
- Specific, not general. "A meeting invite titled Pre-Pre-Standup-Retro Sync,
  described as informal" beats "meetings are bad."
- The corporate register escalates with the absurdity. That contrast is the
  whole comic engine.
- Leonard is sympathetic, never pathetic. The player should recognize
  themselves in him, not look down at him.
- Do **not** describe this as a game about catching people lying. It isn't,
  and a version of the site said so for months before it got fixed.
